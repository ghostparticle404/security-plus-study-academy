## Topic 1 — Threat Actors and Motivations (Day 26)

### Question 1

A security team investigates an intrusion involving custom malware, long-term covert access, and an apparent interest in sensitive government negotiations. Which threat actor category is **most consistent** with these observations?

A. An unskilled attacker seeking notoriety  
B. A nation-state or state-sponsored actor  
C. A negligent employee using shadow IT  
D. A customer submitting a support ticket

### Question 2

An employee intentionally exports a confidential client list to sell to a competitor. How should the employee be classified?

A. External hacktivist  
B. Accidental insider  
C. Malicious insider  
D. State-sponsored investigator

### Question 3

A group publicly claims responsibility for disrupting an organization's website to protest its policies. Which motivation is **most likely**?

A. Ideological or political influence  
B. Routine system maintenance  
C. Authorized vulnerability testing  
D. Accidental misconfiguration

### Question 4

An analyst discovers a ransomware note claiming to come from a well-known criminal group. What is the **best** conclusion?

A. The named group is definitively responsible  
B. The attacker must have nation-state funding  
C. The note proves the attacker's country of origin  
D. The claim is a lead that requires independent corroboration

## Topic 2 — Attack Surfaces and Vectors (Day 27)

### Question 5

A law firm has a public VPN portal, employee laptops, a cloud document repository, and a third-party billing service. What does the term **attack surface** describe?

A. Only the VPN portal's public IP address  
B. All potential points through which an attacker could interact with or compromise the environment  
C. Only systems that have already been compromised  
D. The final business impact of an incident

### Question 6

An attacker sends an employee a malicious attachment that exploits an unpatched document reader. What is the **initial attack vector**?

A. The malicious email attachment  
B. The organization's backup schedule  
C. The confidential file eventually stolen  
D. The employee's job title

### Question 7

A contractor's account can access sensitive files long after the contract ends. Which action would **most directly** reduce this exposure?

A. Increase the length of the company website's footer  
B. Disable audit logging  
C. Revoke unneeded contractor access and review account lifecycle controls  
D. Publish the account's password policy

### Question 8

An attacker compromises a web application, steals a service credential, and then accesses an internal database. Which statement **best** describes this sequence?

A. It is one isolated vulnerability with no subsequent activity  
B. It is a defensive patch-management workflow  
C. It proves the database was publicly exposed  
D. It is an attack path linking initial access to a valuable target

## Topic 3 — Social Engineering and Phishing (Day 28)

### Question 9

A tailored email impersonates a law firm's managing partner and asks a specific paralegal to send confidential case files. What type of attack is this **most specifically**?

A. Spear phishing  
B. Wardriving  
C. Cryptojacking  
D. DNS zone transfer

### Question 10

A caller claims to be from the IT help desk and insists an employee must immediately approve an unexpected MFA prompt to avoid losing access. Which combination **best** describes the technique?

A. Physical tailgating and data masking  
B. Authority impersonation, urgency, and MFA fatigue  
C. Authorized change management and network segmentation  
D. Hash collision and certificate pinning

### Question 11

An email displays `billing@trusted-firm.example` as the sender but directs the user to sign in at `trusted-firm-login.example.net`. What is the **strongest immediate warning sign**?

A. The message mentions billing  
B. The message has a subject line  
C. The destination domain is different from the claimed organization's domain  
D. The sender used a professional signature

### Question 12

An employee receives an unexpected request to change a supplier's bank account details. What is the **best** next step?

A. Reply to the email and ask whether it is genuine  
B. Make the change because the request sounds urgent  
C. Open the attached document to verify its branding  
D. Verify the request through an independently established contact channel

## Topic 4 — Malware and Ransomware (Day 29)

### Question 13

Malicious software spreads automatically between vulnerable computers without requiring users to open infected files. Which type **best** fits this behavior?

A. Worm  
B. Logic bomb  
C. Trojan  
D. Adware

### Question 14

An endpoint repeatedly launches a malicious process after reboot because an unauthorized scheduled task was created. What behavior does the scheduled task demonstrate?

A. Data classification  
B. Persistence  
C. Secure disposal  
D. Nonrepudiation

### Question 15

An attacker steals confidential files before encrypting a company's servers and threatens to publish the files unless payment is made. This is an example of:

A. Full-disk encryption for compliance  
B. A harmless backup verification  
C. Double extortion ransomware  
D. A network availability test

### Question 16

An analyst sees an unfamiliar executable followed by unusual outbound connections and mass file renaming. Which interpretation is **most appropriate**?

A. Each event independently proves a specific malware family  
B. The events should be ignored unless an antivirus alert fires  
C. The connections prove that all data has been exfiltrated  
D. The combined behaviors warrant investigation for possible malicious execution and impact

## Topic 5 — Indicators and Threat Intelligence (Day 30)

### Question 17

A security tool detects a file hash associated with a known malicious sample. What is the hash **primarily** an example of?

A. An indicator of compromise (IoC)  
B. A business continuity objective  
C. A legal attribution finding  
D. An access control policy

### Question 18

An account signs in from an unusual location, immediately requests privileged access, and begins exporting large amounts of data. Why might this sequence be more useful than a single suspicious IP address?

A. It guarantees the user's identity was stolen  
B. It describes potentially malicious behavior across several events  
C. It eliminates the need to investigate false positives  
D. It proves a particular threat group is responsible

### Question 19

A security team wants to exchange structured threat intelligence automatically between compatible tools. Which pair is **most relevant**?

A. DHCP and ARP  
B. SMTP and POP3  
C. STIX and TAXII  
D. WPA3 and 802.1X

### Question 20

A threat intelligence feed reports a suspicious domain. Before blocking it organization-wide, what should an analyst **prioritize**?

A. Treat every feed entry as a confirmed attack  
B. Publish the domain with confidential case details  
C. Attribute the domain to a nation-state immediately  
D. Assess source reliability, context, recency, and relevance to the environment

## Answer key and explanations

**Do not open these sections until you have answered all 20 questions.** Each explanation links to the relevant Week 06 lesson section.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Reveal answers 1–4 — Threat actors and motivations</summary>

**1. B — Nation-state or state-sponsored actor.** Sophisticated tools, sustained access, and intelligence collection are consistent with state-backed activity, but these clues alone do not prove attribution. [Review actor categories](day-26-threat-actors-and-motivations.md#2-common-threat-actor-categories).

**2. C — Malicious insider.** The employee knowingly misuses legitimate access for personal gain. [Review insider threats](day-26-threat-actors-and-motivations.md#insider-threats).

**3. A — Ideological or political influence.** Hacktivism commonly aims to advance a cause or publicize opposition, although motives may overlap. [Review motivations](day-26-threat-actors-and-motivations.md#4-motivations-and-objectives).

**4. D — Requires independent corroboration.** Names, notes, and claimed identities can be copied or fabricated. Attribution requires converging evidence. [Review attribution limitations](day-26-threat-actors-and-motivations.md#why-attribution-is-difficult).

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Reveal answers 5–8 — Attack surfaces and vectors</summary>

**5. B — All potential points of interaction or compromise.** Attack surfaces include systems, accounts, applications, people, vendors, and other exposures. [Review attack surface categories](day-27-attack-surfaces-and-vectors.md#2-what-belongs-on-an-attack-surface).

**6. A — The malicious email attachment.** The vector is the delivery or access route; the unpatched reader is the exploited weakness. [Review attack vectors](day-27-attack-surfaces-and-vectors.md#3-attack-vectors-how-attempts-begin).

**7. C — Revoke unnecessary access.** Removing stale accounts and permissions closes an avoidable identity exposure. [Review prioritization](day-27-attack-surfaces-and-vectors.md#7-prioritize-exposure-reduction).

**8. D — An attack path.** The steps connect an initial entry point with credential misuse and access to a target asset. [Review attack paths](day-27-attack-surfaces-and-vectors.md#5-model-an-attack-path).

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Reveal answers 9–12 — Social engineering and phishing</summary>

**9. A — Spear phishing.** The attack is tailored to a particular recipient and context. [Review phishing variations](day-28-social-engineering-and-phishing.md#2-phishing-and-its-major-variations).

**10. B — Authority impersonation, urgency, and MFA fatigue.** The caller uses a claimed trusted role and time pressure to induce approval of an unsolicited authentication request. [Review MFA fatigue](day-28-social-engineering-and-phishing.md#mfa-fatigue-and-help-desk-impersonation).

**11. C — The destination domain differs.** The actual sign-in destination does not match the claimed organization; visible sender text alone cannot establish legitimacy. [Review URL inspection](day-28-social-engineering-and-phishing.md#reading-a-url-safely).

**12. D — Independent verification.** Confirm financial changes using a previously trusted phone number or another established channel, not contact information supplied in the suspicious message. [Review defensive controls](day-28-social-engineering-and-phishing.md#6-defensive-controls-people-process-and-technology).

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Reveal answers 13–16 — Malware and ransomware</summary>

**13. A — Worm.** Worms are characterized by self-propagation, often by exploiting reachable vulnerable systems. [Review malware types](day-29-malware-and-ransomware.md#2-major-malware-types).

**14. B — Persistence.** A scheduled task that restarts malware after reboot helps maintain attacker access. [Review persistence](day-29-malware-and-ransomware.md#persistence-examples).

**15. C — Double extortion ransomware.** The attacker combines encryption with the threat of disclosing stolen data. [Review ransomware patterns](day-29-malware-and-ransomware.md#4-ransomware-and-extortion).

**16. D — Investigate the combined behaviors.** Correlated execution, network, and file activity may indicate an attack, but do not independently prove a specific family or exfiltration outcome. [Review indicator classification](day-29-malware-and-ransomware.md#5-recognizing-and-classifying-incident-indicators).

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Reveal answers 17–20 — Indicators and threat intelligence</summary>

**17. A — Indicator of compromise.** A known malicious file hash is a technical artifact that may support detection; matches still require context. [Review IoCs and IoAs](day-30-indicators-and-threat-intelligence.md#iocs-versus-ioas).

**18. B — It describes a behavioral sequence.** Multiple related actions can indicate a suspicious pattern even when individual artifacts change. [Review IoCs and IoAs](day-30-indicators-and-threat-intelligence.md#iocs-versus-ioas).

**19. C — STIX and TAXII.** STIX provides a structured representation of threat intelligence, while TAXII supports exchanging it. [Review intelligence sharing](day-30-indicators-and-threat-intelligence.md#6-threat-intelligence-sharing-and-frameworks).

**20. D — Assess reliability and relevance.** Intelligence should be evaluated for accuracy, timeliness, context, and possible operational consequences before action. [Review source quality](day-30-indicators-and-threat-intelligence.md#evaluating-source-quality).

</details>
